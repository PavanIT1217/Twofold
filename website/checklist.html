The family emergency information checklist

If you were in hospital tomorrow, could your partner find the insurance policy, the bank locker key and the password to the electricity account? Here is everything worth writing down, in one place, for the people who would need it.

Updated 13 October 2026 · About 8 minutes to read ·

Most families keep this information in one person's head, a few email inboxes and a cupboard full of files. That works until the person who knows is unwell, travelling or gone. The checklist below is what financial planners, lawyers and families who've been through it recommend recording. You don't need to finish it in one sitting: start with insurance and bank accounts, then add the rest over a few weekends.

1. A "read this first" note

One page, written to your family, that tells them where to begin. It matters more than any single document.

  • The first three things to do, and who to call first.
  • Where the original papers are kept (which cupboard, which locker, which bank branch).
  • Which accounts pay the household bills and which need to be kept funded.
  • Anything that isn't obvious: money lent to a friend, a policy in a maiden name, a second phone number.

2. Insurance policies

Insurance is the most common thing families can't find when they need it. For every policy (life, term, health, vehicle, home, travel):

  • Insurer, policy number and the type of cover.
  • Sum assured and the nominee or beneficiary.
  • Premium amount, due date and how it's paid.
  • Agent's name and number, and the claims helpline.
  • For health cover: the TPA or cashless card, network hospitals and who in the family is covered.
  • A scan of the policy document and where the original is.

3. Bank accounts and lockers

  • Bank, branch, account type and account number for every account, including old ones that are rarely used.
  • Who the joint holders and nominees are.
  • Fixed and recurring deposits: amount, maturity date and nominee.
  • Safe deposit locker: bank, branch, locker number, where the key is kept and who else is authorised.
  • Your relationship manager or branch contact.

4. Investments and retirement

  • Demat and trading accounts: broker, client ID and depository participant.
  • Mutual funds: the folio numbers and the registrar (or the app you invest through).
  • Retirement savings: provident fund, pension accounts (PPF, EPF, NPS, 401(k), IRA or their equivalent) with account numbers and nominees.
  • Gold, bonds, small savings schemes and any physical certificates.
  • Your financial adviser or tax accountant, if you have one.

5. Property and home

  • For each property: address, registration or title number, survey number and whose name it's in.
  • Where the sale deed, title papers and tax receipts are kept.
  • Property tax and society or HOA dues: account numbers and due dates.
  • Rental agreements, tenants' details and the deposit held.
  • Utility accounts: electricity, water, gas, internet, with consumer numbers.
  • Home maintenance contacts: electrician, plumber, the building manager.

6. Identity documents

  • National ID and tax ID numbers for everyone in the family (for example Aadhaar and PAN in India, or Social Security numbers in the US).
  • Passports, with expiry dates.
  • Driving licences, voter IDs, birth and marriage certificates.
  • Scans of each one, so a copy is always to hand.

7. Loans, cards and vehicles

  • Home, car, personal and education loans: lender, loan account number, EMI and end date. Note any loan insurance.
  • Credit cards: bank, the last four digits and the customer care number (never the full card number and CVV).
  • Vehicles: registration certificate, insurance renewal date, pollution certificate and service centre.

8. Passwords and digital accounts

A family member may need to get into an email account to reset a bank password, or into the phone itself to reach a two-factor code.

  • The phone's unlock code and the password manager's master password, if you use one.
  • Main email accounts and how to recover them.
  • Net banking, insurance portals, tax filing and utility logins.
  • Shared streaming, shopping and cloud storage accounts.
  • Social media accounts and what you'd like done with them.

9. People to call

  • Family doctor, specialists, and medical conditions, allergies and medicines.
  • Lawyer, chartered accountant or tax preparer, insurance agent and financial adviser.
  • Employer's HR contact for group insurance, gratuity and pension claims.
  • Close friends or relatives who know your affairs.

10. Will and wishes

  • Whether you have a will, where the original is and who the executor is.
  • Guardianship wishes for children.
  • Organ donation, medical care and funeral wishes.
  • Power of attorney documents.

Where to keep it all

The list above is exactly what a thief would want, so it needs to be kept safely, and it must still be easy for your family to open on a bad day. The common options each fall short somewhere:

  • A paper file is private, but it's in one place, it goes out of date, and nobody can read it from a hospital.
  • A shared spreadsheet or notes app is convenient, but it's usually stored unencrypted on someone else's servers and tied to one person's account.
  • A password manager is great for logins, but isn't designed for policies, nominees, property papers and "what to do" notes.
